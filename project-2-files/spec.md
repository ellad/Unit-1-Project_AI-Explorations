## Goal

Provide a locally running, evidence-led calculator that helps creative-media employees estimate the energy, carbon, and water associated with professional AI use, including image, video, and coding-agent work, and place those estimates in context with selected digital habits. It must support informed conclusions—not a predetermined view of AI—through visible sources, clear calculations, and honest communication of uncertainty.

## Features

### 1. Image and video generation footprint — approved

- **Need and outcome:** Employees, particularly Alex, need professional AI-use estimates that include image and video generation rather than text prompts alone. They can add these outputs to their estimate and see their contribution to the combined footprint.
- **Behavior and inputs:** The usage builder accepts an image-generation entry with an output count. A video-generation entry accepts an output count (number of clips) and a duration in seconds. Resolution is not a control: every video estimate uses the fixed 720p reference configuration from the available Veo 3.1 evidence. Each entry also uses the project-wide retry/regeneration multiplier once that feature is specified.
- **Outputs and calculation basis:** Image entries display estimated energy, carbon, and water as a single point estimate. Energy uses Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell's own published mean across their eight tested Stable-Diffusion-family models (2.907 Wh/image) as a proxy for commercial image tools. Luccioni et al. do not publish an averaged carbon figure — only per-model extremes (their most carbon-intensive tested model emits roughly sixteen times what their least-intensive model does) — so carbon is derived from that average energy figure using the calculator's own selected-location grid factor, the same treatment already used for video and text carbon. Water is derived from the existing NREL electricity-to-water factor. Video entries display estimated energy and carbon as a min–max range. For each clip, the calculator scales the 19.8–43.4 Wh GPU/accelerator estimate for a Veo 3.1 8-second, 720p reference clip by `entered duration ÷ 8 seconds`, then grosses the result up to facility energy using Google's published 1.09 PUE and converts carbon with the calculator's existing grid-intensity assumption. Linear duration scaling is a project modeling assumption, not a result published by the Sustainable AI Group paper; the paper is the source only for the 8-second reference range, inferred indirectly from API response times and an assumed hardware platform. Water is a single derived point estimate from the existing NREL electricity-to-water factor, the same treatment used for the image entry, since neither the video paper nor EcoLogits publish a water figure for this estimate. Both entry types contribute to the calculator's combined totals and comparison views.
- **Uncertainty and interface:** The interface must identify the image estimate as an open-model proxy, and must distinguish the image estimate's two figures by confidence: energy is Luccioni et al.'s own published average, while carbon and water are both the calculator's own derivations — carbon from that average energy using the selected-location grid factor (since Luccioni et al. publish only per-model carbon extremes, not an average), water from the existing NREL electricity-to-water factor. It must explain that the video energy/carbon range is inferred indirectly — from real Veo 3.1 API response times and an assumed hardware platform, not direct measurement — by a paper still under peer review, and label the video water value as derived. It must state plainly that duration is scaled linearly by the calculator as an assumption, while 720p remains fixed; the estimate is not a claim about other resolutions, every clip length, or video-generation providers generally. It must also explain why the image estimate is a single number while the video estimate is a range: the image proxy source reports per-model extremes rather than a Monte Carlo distribution, so the calculator averages rather than ranges, keeping the image estimate comparable to a single representative output. Inputs must have clear units and validation that prevents negative or incomplete values from entering the total. Explanations of sources, calculations, and limitations must be written in plain, non-technical language.
- **Acceptance checks:** Adding image outputs changes all combined totals; changing video output count or duration changes the video and combined totals; no resolution control exists; doubling a valid video duration doubles its energy, carbon, and derived-water estimate; video estimates use the fixed Veo 3.1 720p reference and visibly label linear duration scaling as a calculator assumption; the video estimate displays a min–max range consistent with the existing text-model convention, while the image estimate remains a single point estimate; the image estimate labels energy as Luccioni's own published average and both carbon and water as calculator-derived; the relevant sources, proxy/derived labels, and limitations are visible where users can inspect the estimate; and all source, calculation, and limitation explanations for this feature are written in plain, non-technical language.

### 2. Digital-life activity comparisons — approved

- **Need and outcome:** Employees need to compare estimated AI use with familiar digital activities, rather than only physical-world activities. Alex, Jordan, and Robin can see how streaming, video calls, gaming, and social-media use relate to their AI estimate without treating any activity as inherently good or bad.
- **Behavior and inputs:** The calculator provides four separate, independently adjustable daily-hour inputs: streaming, video calls, gaming, and social media. Changing one input changes that activity's own subtotal and the combined digital-life comparison total, without changing the values entered for the other three activities.
- **Outputs and calculation basis:** Each activity displays estimated daily energy, carbon, and derived water use, with yearly figures derived using the calculator's existing daily/yearly framing. Streaming, video calls, and social media each display a single point estimate; gaming displays a min–max range, reflecting the "Hot Games" paper's console-to-desktop-PC wattage split (160–305 W) rather than an invented single blended value. Each activity also contributes to a combined digital-life comparison total. Streaming uses IEA's estimate, including IEA's own published carbon figure; video calls use Mytton's published bandwidth-based energy estimate; gaming uses the "Hot Games" device-wattage range; and social media uses the project's own back-of-envelope estimate derived from Greenspector's measured TikTok battery draw (15.81 mAh/hour) and a standard nominal Li-ion battery voltage (3.7 V). None of video calls, gaming, or social media has a published carbon figure, so each activity's carbon is derived from its energy using the calculator's selected-location grid factor, the same treatment used for image and video carbon. Water for all four is derived with the NREL electricity-to-water factor.
- **Uncertainty and interface:** The comparison category must remain distinct from AI-use totals and identify source confidence and limitations for each activity: streaming's dated global baseline; video-call undercounting of data-centre and device energy plus the calculator's own assumption of a 1:1 call (Mytton's figure scales with participant count and camera use, which the hours-only input does not capture); gaming's crude, console-versus-PC-only hardware assumptions (mobile gaming is excluded entirely, matching the source's own exclusion); and social media as the calculator's own estimate, based on one measured app (TikTok) on one 2021 device, not a social-media-wide average or an external study finding. Video-call, gaming, and social-media carbon values must be labeled as calculator-derived from energy via the selected grid factor, since none of those sources publish a carbon figure directly; streaming's carbon retains its directly-published IEA figure. Derived water values must be labeled as such. Explanations of sources, calculations, and limitations must be written in plain, non-technical language.
- **Acceptance checks:** Each of the four inputs can be changed independently; each change updates only the relevant activity subtotal and the digital-life combined total; all four activities are visible alongside the existing comparisons; gaming displays a min–max range while streaming, video calls, and social media display single point estimates; sources, derivation labels, and limitations (including the 1:1 video-call assumption and gaming's console/PC-only hardware basis) are available to inspect; and all source, calculation, and limitation explanations for this feature are written in plain, non-technical language.

### 3. Coding/agent session intensity and project total — approved

- **Need and outcome:** Jordan and other employees with long, uneven coding-agent workflows need a project-level estimate that does not reduce every session to one fixed benchmark or force it into a daily average. They can model a project as the actual mix of distinct sessions and see one summed total.
- **Behavior and inputs:** A project builder lets users add multiple coding/agent-session entries. Each entry accepts a lines-of-code value and is kept distinct from the others; users can change or remove an entry before reviewing the project total. The calculator converts each entry to an estimated token count using its existing rough conversion: `lines × 10 tokens per line ÷ 15% code fraction`.
- **Outputs and calculation basis:** The builder shows each session's estimated footprint and one summed project total for energy, carbon, and water. The total remains separate from the calculator's per-day/per-year aggregate. The comparison bar graph shows the project's total carbon and water values as clearly labeled project-scope items, rather than translating them into activity hours. Its calculations use the existing EcoLogits per-token methodology after conversion, rather than mixing in Couch's separate energy methodology. It shows two reference anchors for context: approximately 1,500 lines for EcoLogits' 100,000-token standard benchmark and approximately 8,900 lines for Couch's ~592,000-token self-logged session.
- **Uncertainty and interface:** The lines-to-token conversion must be labeled as a rough estimate. The standard anchor must be described as a fixed content-length benchmark, not typical-session data; the heavy anchor must be identified as one person's self-logged data (n=1), not a representative average. The comparison graph must make the project's time scope explicit so a project total is not mistaken for a daily figure. Users must be able to inspect these limitations and the calculation method, explained in plain, non-technical language.
- **Acceptance checks:** Users can add, edit, and remove distinct session entries; changing one entry updates its estimate and the summed project total; the graph includes clearly labeled project-total carbon and water items; the two reference anchors are visible and correctly characterized; the project calculation uses the documented lines-to-token conversion and EcoLogits basis; and all source, calculation, and limitation explanations for this feature are written in plain, non-technical language.

### 4. Home Wi-Fi/router baseline — approved

- **Need and outcome:** Employees need context for the always-on network infrastructure that supports their digital activities. All three profiles can compare AI and activity estimates with an adjustable daily home-router/gateway baseline without implying that it is caused by one particular activity.
- **Behavior and inputs:** The calculator provides a separately adjustable assumed router/gateway wattage, defaulting to 6.5 W. It treats the device as operating for 24 hours per day, not as a sum of the Feature 2 activity hours.
- **Outputs and calculation basis:** The calculator converts the entered wattage to daily energy, carbon, and derived water use, and shows the result as a distinct digital-infrastructure comparison. It must not contribute to Feature 2's combined activity total, preventing double counting. The 6.5 W default is based on a current EU Joint Research Centre allowance for a Wi-Fi 6 gateway.
- **Uncertainty and interface:** The interface identifies this as an always-on baseline rather than the incremental cost of an individual activity. It provides the adjustable wattage input, clear units, the underlying source rationale, and an explanation that the current EU allowance is not a U.S. field measurement and that router/gateway configurations vary. Water is labeled as derived with the NREL factor. Explanations of sources, calculations, and limitations must be written in plain, non-technical language.
- **Acceptance checks:** Changing router wattage updates only the router comparison and its derived daily/yearly values; activity-hour inputs do not alter it; it is not included in the Feature 2 combined total; its source rationale and derived-water label are inspectable; and all source, calculation, and limitation explanations for this feature are written in plain, non-technical language.

### 5. Retries/regenerations multiplier — approved (revised 2026-09-23, needs re-approval)

- **Need and outcome:** Employees, especially people iterating on creative outputs, need their estimate to include retries and regenerations that the original calculator excluded. They can state their own iteration count without the calculator pretending that research provides a typical default.
- **Behavior and inputs:** Every text, image, and video entry has its own adjustable retry/regeneration multiplier. It defaults to 1, meaning the original output is counted once; a value greater than 1 represents the total number of generations the employee estimates they used for that entry. Coding/agent-session entries do not have a retry multiplier: a session's lines-of-code figure already describes one full run, and a repeated run of an entire agent session rarely reproduces the same amount of code, unlike regenerating a short text, image, or video output. An employee who re-ran a whole session represents that as its own separate session with its own line count instead.
- **Outputs and calculation basis:** The multiplier scales the entry's estimated energy, carbon, and water before it contributes to its relevant subtotal or combined AI-use total. It does not change values entered in other rows.
- **Uncertainty and interface:** The interface must state that no credible evidence supports a universal or average retry count. Any multiplier above 1 is the employee's own estimate of their behavior, not a research-backed value. Inputs must prevent values below 1 and clearly explain that the original generation is included, in plain, non-technical language. The interface must also explain, in plain language, why coding sessions are excluded from the multiplier.
- **Acceptance checks:** Every applicable entry (text, image, video) exposes a multiplier defaulting to 1; increasing it scales only that entry and its associated totals; an invalid value below 1 cannot enter the calculation; the no-default-evidence limitation is visible to inspect; coding sessions expose no retry control at all, and the reason is inspectable; and all source, calculation, and limitation explanations for this feature are written in plain, non-technical language.

## Out of scope

- Estimating AI-model training impacts, which the original calculator excludes and the approved research does not supply a compatible use-based methodology for.
- A comparison with a supposedly non-AI web search, because AI Overviews make that baseline unreliable.
- A personal-device electricity baseline for laptops, desktops, or televisions, because the research did not establish an appropriate active-use source.
- Social-media figures presented as a published external footprint finding; the feature uses only the calculator's clearly labeled derived estimate.
- A research-backed default retry rate; no credible source was found.

### Input-based carbon and water pie charts

- **Purpose:** Give employees a clear proportional view of the carbon or water associated with the calculator's new inputs, without conflating them with the broader lifestyle-footprint controls.
- **Behavior:** A pie chart can switch between carbon and water and breaks down only the new inputs: AI-use entries, the four digital-life activities, and the router baseline. Users can select individual, unique coding/agent-session entries from the project builder to include once in the daily-use pie for the day those sessions occurred.

Slices must have accessible labels, values, and a text alternative so the information does not depend on color or the chart alone.
